What the job is

The van's charter is ski base camp through Summit, Eagle and Teton winters, so this is a cold-side problem: keep heat in, keep the steel dry, and keep the cab from fogging. Four things set what insulation can do:

  • The ribs conduct around it. Every wall rib and roof bow is a steel path from inside to out. Filling the cavities to R 5 to 8 gives a wall that performs more like R 3 to 4 overall; a house wall is R 13 to 20. Past filling the cavities, more R-value buys little.
  • Glass loses more than walls. The windshield and two door windows are single-pane and uninsulated. Covers on them matter more than another inch anywhere else.
  • Condensation is the enemy. Warm breath meets cold steel behind the panels and runs down to the floor seams. The fix is insulation that does not hold water (hydrophobic Thinsulate, or wool that buffers it), no plastic vapor barrier that traps it against the skin, and the Fan-Tastic vent running low while sleeping.
  • The heater does the work. Insulation sets how long the heater runs; it does not replace it. The charter's heat reference is a Webasto Air Top 2000. That decision is outside this report.

The roof is also the hottest surface in summer, where the same insulation plus the fan carry the day.

What Ford's manual says

The Transit Body and Equipment Mounting Manual (2020 North America edition) has no section on cargo-area thermal insulation. Three things in it still apply:

  • Wiring runs behind the walls and roof bows in the "precautionary drill zones" (section 4.1.12). Insulation goes in around the harnesses, never over a connector, and nothing gets stapled or screwed through them.
  • Fixings into the lining "may be inadequate" (5.6.1), so insulation cannot be the thing that holds a panel; the panel holds to furring on the ribs and the insulation sits behind it.
  • Materials near the engine bay must take -40°F to 257°F (4.1); inside the cargo area the limits are the products' own. 3M rates SM600L to 120°C (248°F); expanded PVC and acrylic in the lining report are limited to 140°F and 160°F.

Fire: FMVSS 302 (burn no faster than 102 mm per minute) governs interior materials in new vehicles and is the benchmark here. Thinsulate SM600L carries a self-extinguishing rating to it on 3M's data sheet. Foam plastics (XPS, EPS, polyiso, spray foam) carry building-code ratings instead and are meant to sit behind a barrier, which in this van is the plywood lining.

Install order and keeping it dry

Insulation goes in after the wiring and before the lining, and nothing here needs a drill.

  1. Measure the cavities. Rib depth at three wall heights, roof bow depth at two bows and the center span, and the floor corrugation depth. Write them on the weight budget page. (20 minutes.)
  2. Wash the steel. Isopropyl on every panel the butyl or adhesive will touch. (30 minutes.)
  3. Butyl on the big flat panels. One 18 sq ft Kilmat box, torn into palm-sized pieces, rolled onto the center of each roof section between bows and each quarter panel, 25% coverage. Knock on the panel; when it thuds instead of rings, stop. (1 hour.)
  4. Run the wiring. Every circuit the electrical plan calls for, in split loom, before anything covers the ribs. Leave a pull string in each run.
  5. Thinsulate, ceiling first. Spray 3M 90 (or the adhesive the retailer bundles) on the steel, press the 60" width up between the walls, then stuff strips into each bow cavity. Walls next, cavities first, then the face. Scissors cut it; no mask needed. (Half a day.)
  6. Doors. Thin strips in the door skins, glued, clear of the latch and the check arm. (1 hour.)
  7. Floor. 1/2" XPS laid on the corrugation ridges, then the 3/8" subfloor from the lining report over it; the subfloor's own lashing-point anchors hold both. No glue to the floor pan. (2 hours.)
  8. Lining. Furring and panels per the lining report.

Moisture, in three rules. No plastic vapor barrier anywhere: a van skin is already a vapor barrier, and a second one on the warm side traps breath between the two. Choose insulation that sheds water (Thinsulate) or buffers it (wool) so a wet night dries out through the panel gaps. And crack the Fan-Tastic vent low while sleeping, because two people exhale about a liter of water a night and it has to leave the van somewhere.

Thinsulate wins over wool on two of those. Wool is 0.2 lb/sq ft to Thinsulate's 0.14, needs the full 2", and holds water it then has to release; Thinsulate's fibers are hydrophobic and it comes back out intact. Wool wins on R-value and on fire rating (Class A against self-extinguishing), and it is the pick if the measured cavities turn out deeper than 2".

Open items

  • Measure the cavities (walls at three heights, roof bows, floor corrugation) before ordering.
  • Confirm 3M 90 is the adhesive the roll ships with or buy it; the two-can count is an estimate.
  • Decide Stealth or Reflective for the cab bundle.
  • Heater choice is the other half of this question and lives outside this report.
  • Polyiso is not stocked locally; if a 1" board is wanted for a flat section, a lumber yard order is required and the cold-weather derating has to be weighed.
