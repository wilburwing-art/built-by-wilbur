Do these first:

  1. Decide whether the shower zone gets StarBoard XL; it sets one wall panel's size.
  2. Measure the real panel areas (the figures here are derived from published van dimensions).
  3. Buy one 1/5" underlayment sheet ($22.53) and bend it against the roof curve before ordering the rest.

What Ford says about lining the cargo area

Ford's own guidance is thin plywood, pre-drilled and machine cut, fixed without drilling the floor. From the Transit Body and Equipment Mounting Manual, 2020 North America edition, sections 5.6.1 and 5.6.2 (the 2016 edition was not read):

  • "It is recommended to use 9mm thickness for floors and 6mm thickness for side and door lining." Plywood should be water resistant (WBP, water and boil proof).
  • "Do not drill through floor panels, use existing load lashing points when securing the panels." A plywood floor should be joint free, with aluminum floor trims.
  • Panels should be precision cut by machine, not by hand jigsaw, and pre-drilled.
  • "Varnish or paint wooden interior cargo area panels if exposed to high humidity conditions."
  • "Fixing to the lining material may be inadequate for normal safe operation of the vehicle." Plan through-bolting for anything heavy, like racking or a bed, before the lining goes on.
  • Extra weight on the doors may need reinforcement at the hinge and check arm.
  • Check the "No Drill Zones" and wiring routing figures before any hole in the walls or doors.

Ford does not name a fire standard for lining in this section. FMVSS 302 (burn no faster than 102 mm per minute) is the federal rule for interior materials in new vehicles and the benchmark used in this report (49 CFR 571.302).

Notes on the wall: Ford says fixings into the lining "may be inadequate" for anything that matters, so pockets and lights go into the ply and anything heavier goes through to a rib. Floor

The floor is two layers: a plywood subfloor that spans the corrugations and takes point loads, and a wear surface on top. Ford's number for the subfloor is 9 mm, water-and-boil-proof, in one joint-free piece, held at the factory tie-down points rather than by holes in the floor pan.

How it all attaches

The lining hangs on the body; nothing heavy hangs on the lining.

  1. Furring first. Screw or rivnut 1/2" plywood strips (Vans Are Swell includes them; Home Depot 1/2" BCX 2 x 4 at $28.85 cuts into about ten) to the wall ribs and roof bows, outside Ford's wiring and no-drill zones. The panels then screw to the furring, so every panel screw is in wood.
  2. Pre-drill and machine-cut the panels as Ford says. A circular saw with a plywood blade, or a track saw, for the long cuts; a jigsaw only for the wheel-well notches.
  3. Ceiling panels in two or three pieces across the van so each piece bends to the roof curve. Fabric hides the joints.
  4. Wall panels in sections that match the rib spacing, with the lower panel removable where the galley and bed frames bolt through to the body.
  5. Floor: cut the 3/8" subfloor in two pieces with the joint under the galley, lay it dry, mark the tie-down points from below, and bolt through them with countersunk bolts. Vinyl goes down with the maker's adhesive or loose-laid under the trims.
  6. Shower panel in slotted holes with stainless screws and washers, with a bead of sealant along the floor edge.
  7. Grommets wherever a wire passes through sheet metal, and a windscreen-type sealer, per Ford.

Thread locker on any bolt into aluminum, and a drive on a rough road before the fabric goes on, so loose screws are found while they are still reachable.
